A boundary row is worth what the model can do with it

The previous round showed the boundary row was not the limiting factor on a curved wall — a constant gradient cannot satisfy it — and named the closing question: the quadratic with the row, where the row should now be satisfiable.
And the first thing it found corrects a sentence I shipped last round. v3647 wrote "the row is already exact at its own quadrature point." That was two claims wearing one sentence: the normal is exact at the arc midpoint, to round-off — but the point is not on the wall at all. A chord's midpoint lies inside the circle by a(1 − cos(π/nt)), a closed form, O(h²). An exact direction at an inexact location.

1. Two claims, one sentence

So the row is satisfiable to O(h²) rather than exactly — against the constant gradient's O(h) residual. The quadratic's obstacle is the geometry of the face; the linear one's was the model.

2. With and without

The row now helps, by a steady 40% — ratios 0.590 / 0.686 / 0.647 / 0.619 / 0.603, converging to a constant. But it buys a constant factor, not an order: 1.92 against 1.88. It moves the line down and does not tilt it, which is what an O(h²)-satisfiable constraint on an O(h²) method must do.

3. Seven rounds in one sentence

A boundary row helps exactly to the extent the reconstruction can satisfy it. Perfectly, not at all, or to second order — and a fourth case that isn't on the scale, where the model satisfies a wrong thing and lands worse than refusing. The row was never a free correction and never a fault. It is a constraint, and a constraint is worth what the model can do with it.
