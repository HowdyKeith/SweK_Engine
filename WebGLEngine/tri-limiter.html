Triangles — where the argument finally does not run

Two rounds proved a limiter monotone and each ended by naming the same untested case, in as many words: on triangles the vertex offsets are not ½ and the argument does not run. Both proofs rested on one geometric fact — the offset from the cell centre to the furthest evaluation point is exactly half the lever the gradient was measured on. That is a property of the square, not of the limiter.
So measure the number first. Centroid to furthest vertex, over centroid to nearest neighbouring centroid: on a square, 0.5. On the regular right-triangle mesh, √(5/2) = 1.5811 — from centroid (2h/3, h/3), far vertex at h√5/3, sibling centroid at h√2/3. Above one, and 3.16× the Cartesian value. A gradient bounded by the neighbour difference is then applied over a lever longer than the one it was measured on. The escape is forced by geometry.

1. The mesh, and where the reconstruction leaves its neighbourhood

The same instrument read zero twice on Cartesian meshes and reads 1288 here, which is what makes it a finding rather than a new detector finding what it was built to find. Barth–Jespersen restores the zero — and the residue is one ulp, the same "bound attained, zero margin" signature minmod showed in 2D, arrived at by a completely different construction.

2. Which centre conserves

Only the centroid. The integral of (x − xcentroid) over a triangle is exactly zero, so the gradient term cancels for any gradient. Expand about the bounding-box middle or a vertex and conservation is gone by fourteen orders. "Centre" is a word a triangle answers at least four ways and only one of them conserves — and it is a different fact from the Cartesian rounds, where conservation came from symmetric subcell offsets. Same conclusion, unrelated reasons, and only this one survives a triangle.
