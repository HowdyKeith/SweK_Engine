The furnace scene should be boring. A diffuse sphere of albedo ρ inside a white emitting environment must render to a flat field of exactly ρ — featureless, not invisible. At ρ = 0.18 you see a flat grey disc against a white sky, because the sphere reflects 18% and the environment emits 100%; only at ρ = 1 does it truly vanish. What must be absent is shading — no bright side, no dark side, no gradient from edge to centre. That is the test every one of these modules was graded against before this page existed, and it is the one picture where seeing nothing is the correct result. If the sphere is visible in that scene, something is wrong.

The other two scenes are there because the furnace one is exact: with a constant sky and a convex sphere, every path escapes on its first bounce and the image has no noise at all. Turn the sky into a gradient, or put a second sphere in the way, and the grain appears — that grain is Monte Carlo error, and it falls as 1/√N.

Ray-marching the blob — a different renderer, on purpose

The tracer above only knows spheres: intersect() loops raySphere and nothing else, so it cannot see a mesh, a voxel volume or a marching-cubes surface. A blob is a metaball field, and the honest way to trace one is to walk the field itself. Press the button and this marches physics/render/sdfMarch.mjs through a two-ball Wyvill field, then checks every hit against the surface marchingTets extracts from the same field — two independent routes to one surface.

Max step is a trade and it does not go away. The Newton estimate |f − iso|/|∇f| is only a distance where the field is locally sloped, and Wyvill falloff flattens to exactly zero outside each ball — so the estimate is degenerate right where a ray arrives. Clamping it is what lets the tracer cross that dead zone; the cost is that a feature thinner than the clamp can be stepped over. The overshoot is bounded by one step, and it is always late, never early.

Does this material conserve energy?

The furnace above grades a Lambertian surface, where the answer is the albedo. That is not what the white furnace test is for in practice. Its real job is this: put a GGX microfacet surface in the same white room with F = 1 and no absorption anywhere, and ask how much light comes back. A conserving model returns exactly 1. Single-scattering GGX — what almost every renderer ships — returns less, and the shortfall is the energy that scattering between microfacets would have given back.

The smooth end is the dangerous end. At roughness 0.05 the surface loses 0.3% — inside any tolerance anybody would write. At roughness 1.0 the same model at a knob setting somebody will use loses about two thirds, and still looks like a plausible rough metal. The plant is the same shape: a masking function borrowed from a different microfacet distribution leaves the lobe normalised, leaves the picture plausible, and breaks the weak furnace test — by 0.7% at roughness 0.1 and 31% at 1.0.

Fresnel — the term the furnace test above deliberately switches off

Everything in the energy test above runs with F = 1. That is not a simplification, it is the only way the deficit means anything: with a real Fresnel term the missing light has been transmitted, not lost, and a correct model would look like it was haemorrhaging energy. The two checks everybody writes catch nothing. Normal incidence and grazing are where anyone verifies a Fresnel term by hand, and the plant is bit-identical at both — the two polarisations coincide at normal incidence and both go to 1 edge-on. Only Brewster's angle notices, and it notices by exactly a factor of two. Schlick's approximation, which most real-time renderers ship, is exact at both ends too — and has no polarisation in it, so Brewster's zero does not blur, it does not exist.

The same BRDF on the GPU — and the one line that only matters there

Every gate behind this page runs in float64. GLSL highp is IEEE binary32, and one expression in GGX is a difference of numbers near 1: cos²(a²−1)+1. The engine now writes it as (1−cos²)+a²cos² instead — the same number, a sum of positives. In double precision the two are indistinguishable, so nothing on the CPU could ever have told them apart. This asks your GPU. It draws no picture: each pixel is one (roughness, cos) pair and the value itself is the output, read back as floats and compared against the CPU module.

Three ways to light a rough surface, and none of them wins everywhere

A rough sphere under a small light can be lit by sampling the light and evaluating the BRDF, by sampling the BRDF and hoping to hit the light, or by doing both and weighting them. All three must give the same answer — only the noise may differ. The measurement below is the noise, and the point is that the winner changes with the regime: a near-mirror finds a big light easily and almost never finds a small one, and a rough surface is the other way round.

The claim for MIS is not that it wins. It is rarely the best cell and it is never the worst — its worst case on this grid is about 7e-3 against 1e-1 for light sampling and 3e-1 for BRDF sampling. That is insurance, not an optimisation, and it matters because a renderer picks one strategy for a whole scene and cannot know which cell each pixel is in. On a diffuse surface the same comparison came out the other way, because a diffuse BRDF has no lobe for the second route to be good at.

Colour — and why the obvious check cannot decide it

Radiance was a scalar through this whole arc. The key everybody reaches for is that a grey world must return identical values per channel — true, exact, and not sufficient, because a renderer that ignored colour entirely would satisfy it. The check that decides is the other one: channel k of a coloured render must equal a monochrome render of that channel's own value, bit for bit. The answer key was already built — ten rounds of graded scalar renderer, run three times.

Stratified sampling — and the pixel where it does nothing

Jittering samples inside an n×n grid instead of drawing them freely is the oldest trick in Monte Carlo rendering. The thing worth measuring is not the error — any error shrinks if you throw samples at it — it is the convergence exponent: independent samples fall as N−1/2 whatever you do, and a better sampler shows up as a steeper slope. Below, both pixels are measured, because stratifying a dimension only helps where the variance lives in that dimension.

The centre pixel is the trap. It is roughly thirty times noisier than the edge pixel, and every bit of that noise is path variance — the bounce directions — which stratifying the pixel domain cannot touch. With a single bounce it has no variance at all, so the exponent there is not small, it does not exist. The pixel that looks like the obvious place to measure is both the loudest and the one where the answer is flat.

The oracle — giving render-QA a right answer instead of a threshold

Every check in render-qa/checks.mjs compares a picture against a number somebody chose, because there was nothing to compare it to. A converged path trace is something to compare it to. Below, a Lambertian sphere is lit from a swept angle and the real terminatorBow check runs on it — beside the closed form the geometry predicts: the terminator is the great circle perpendicular to the light, so it projects to x = R cos θ √(1−(y/R)²). Nothing in the renderer is told that curve.
