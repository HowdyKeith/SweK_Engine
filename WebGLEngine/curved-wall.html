A third category: true, and badly written down

Four rounds had two kinds of boundary assertion: one that is true (exact, free, rank-restoring) and one that is false (worse than the refusal). This is a third — grad·n = 0 holds pointwise on a curved wall, and a single row per face asserts one number for a condition that varies across it.
And the discriminator needs no tolerance: a discretisation error converges under refinement and a false assertion does not. Both cases sit on the same mesh, in the same field — potential flow past a cylinder, φ = U(r + a²/r)cosθ, whose radial derivative is exactly zero at r = a at every angle and nonzero at r = b.

1. The fixture, checked first

The false wall is not uniformly false: grad·n vanishes at θ = π/2 and 3π/2, the top and bottom of the circle. My first check asserted the minimum was large and went red — the check was wrong, not the physics. It now measures the typical magnitude with the two zeros named.

2. The discriminator

On the true curved wall the error converges, approaching first order — the row is a one-number statement of a condition that varies across the face, the variation is O(h), and the damage vanishes. On the false wall it is flat at ~0.5 across a sixteenfold refinement. The same cells with no row converge perfectly well, so the flatness is the row and not the corner. 0.93 against −0.02.

3. What the row actually buys on a curve

Neutral to marginally negative. The same condition on a flat wall measured 1.8e-15 — exact, free and rank-restoring. Curvature destroys the exactness and leaves the cost. So the three categories are not points on one scale: the first is about algebra, the second about resolution, the third about the problem.
