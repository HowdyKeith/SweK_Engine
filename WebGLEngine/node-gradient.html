The third declaration — and the fourth hiding inside it

The previous round found cell-based Green–Gauss consistent but never exact on a skewed mesh. The standard answer is node-based Green–Gauss: read the face value from its two endpoint nodes, which lie on the face, instead of from two centroids which do not.
But a node of a cell-average field has no value until somebody invents one, and that is a fourth declaration nobody names. "Node-based Green–Gauss is linearity-preserving" turns out to be a claim about the node interpolation and not about Green–Gauss at all.

1. Four spellings of "the gradient"

With a least-squares node value it is exact at every skew. With inverse-distance node values it is about 15× better than cell-based and still five orders above exact — an inverse-distance mean reproduces a linear field only if the surrounding cells are symmetric about the node, which a regular mesh's interior is and a sheared one is not.

2. The filter itself was the finding

What counts as "interior" depends on what the scheme reads, not on what the cell has. My first filter used the neighbour count — three faces, three neighbours, interior. But a node-based scheme reads its values from the nodes, so a cell with three interior faces can still be fed a value invented at a boundary vertex from a one-sided stencil. Under the loose filter, inverse-distance reads 32% on a mesh with no shear at all; under the strict one, 1.57e-14. Fourteen orders, entirely from which cells were called interior.
The cell-based schemes never notice, because they never look at a node — which is exactly why this could sit in the fixture through the previous round without anything going red. And the least-squares node value is unaffected by the filter: a one-sided stencil is a fine stencil for a plane fit and a terrible one for a distance-weighted mean. The two node interpolations differ by thirteen orders at the boundary and are indistinguishable in the interior.
