Two gradients, one exact key

Least-squares and Green–Gauss are both called "the gradient", both ship in real solvers, and nothing here had ever compared them. The key needs no tolerance and no reference run: any gradient worth the name reproduces a linear field exactly. Feed u = a + bx + cy and the answer is (b, c) — on every mesh, at every skew, with nothing to converge.
The control comes first. On the regular mesh all three must be exact, because there is nothing to be skewed by. My first Green–Gauss divided by 2A instead of A and this line read exactly 0.5000 on every mesh including that one. An error that is exactly one half at zero skew is a normalisation, not a finding — and the round would otherwise have reported a factor of two as a defect in a standard method.

1. Shear the mesh; the field is still linear

Least-squares is exact at every skew — it solves for the plane through the neighbours, and when the data lie on a plane the residual is zero by construction. Green–Gauss is not. Its face value is the simple average of two cell values, which silently assumes the face midpoint lies halfway between the two centroids — true on a uniform Cartesian grid, false on a triangle, and further from true the more the mesh is sheared. The "improved" distance weighting is about 40% better and still five orders above least-squares.

2. Two instruments, and only one can answer

It converges at second order, so Green–Gauss is consistent — just never exact. It is not broken; it gets a linear field wrong by O(h²) at every finite resolution while least-squares gets it right at all of them.
And on a smooth field the two are indistinguishable — same order, within a quarter in magnitude. A convergence study would call them equivalent and ship either. The exact key separates them by five orders. Both instruments are honest; only one of them can answer this question.
