A circular current loop — the field off the axis

Every textbook gives the field on the axis of a current loop, because that one is elementary. The field everywhere else is one of the few exact off-axis solutions in electromagnetism, and it is written in complete elliptic integrals: with A=(a+r)²+z², B=(a-r)²+z², m=4ar/A,
Bz = μ0I / (2π√A) · [ K(m) + E(m)(a²-r²-z²)/B ]
Br = μ0Iz / (2πr√A) · [ -K(m) + E(m)(a²+r²+z²)/B ]
The parameter is m, not the modulus k. Half the literature writes these with k where k² = 4ar/A, and the two conventions give different fields. The picture below is why that is dangerous rather than merely wrong.

Half-plane: the loop is the bright dot on the right, the axis is the left edge. Colour is log|B|. The difference map is black all the way up the axis and blazes everywhere else.
Look at the left edge of the third panel. On the axis r = 0 forces m = 0, and m = k² has fixed points at exactly 0 and 1 — so both conventions give K = E = π/2 and the two fields are byte-identical there. The mutant reproduces the elementary on-axis law, the centre value μ0I/2a, and the on-axis far-field dipole to the same digits as the correct field. It is wrong only where nobody has an elementary answer to check against.

3. The identity that catches it

div B = (1/r)∂(rBr)/∂r + ∂Bz/∂z — an identity of nature with no reference value at all, so nothing can be tuned to satisfy it
The correct field's residue is the central-difference stencil, not the field: halve h and it falls by four, every time. The mutant sits five to eight orders above that floor. This is the check nobody reaches for first and the only one of the five that a wrong convention cannot survive — because a field that is not a magnetic field has nowhere to hide from an identity.
